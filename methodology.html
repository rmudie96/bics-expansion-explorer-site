Methodology

How the constituency savings estimates are calculated, and how they should be interpreted.

This note explains the approach in plain language. The estimates describe the financial potential of two hypothetical expansions of the British Industrial Competitiveness Scheme (BICS). They are not observations of business bills or forecasts of claims.

1. What is being estimated?

The BICS Expansion Explorer estimates the additional annual electricity bill savings businesses in each of Great Britain's 632 parliamentary constituencies could receive if BICS eligibility were expanded. Production figures are quantified subtotals with explicit coverage gaps, rather than a complete costing of every production activity.

We compare extending eligibility to all manufacturing industries (SIC Section C), or to all production industries: mining and quarrying, manufacturing, electricity and gas supply, and water and waste services (Sections B–E). Existing eligible activities are retained in both cases.

The model combines national electricity statistics with local workplace employment and regional economic data. It uses the 2024 parliamentary constituency boundaries and estimates potential savings, rather than individual businesses' claims.

National costing and the Make UK comparison

These are model estimates with documented coverage and allocation limitations. It measures additional annual electricity bill relief above current BICS and overlapping EII exemptions, at an assumed £40/MWh. It does not calculate the net cost to the Exchequer after funding changes, administration or future tax receipts.

The old £704m manufacturing baseline assumed all remaining consumption in listed industries was already covered. It was not a government spending figure. We have replaced that assumption with the published 2027/28 BICS forecast, so additional savings can arise within listed industries.

Make UK’s May 2026 report gives £3.3bn as the annual cost of its expanded scheme and proposes £40/MWh. Dividing those numbers implies 82.5 TWh, but that is arithmetic, not a consumption dataset published in the report. The report does not show a full bridge from electricity consumption to its cost or an explicit deduction of current BICS. Our starting point before existing support is about £2.48bn; our additional relief after support is about £1.395bn. The earlier official data provide a useful cross-check: DUKES2025 reported 82.2TWh of UK industrial consumption for 2024, including other generation. At £40/MWh this rounds to £3.3bn. It is a broader scope and earlier data vintage than our GB manufacturing grid reference, and is numerically compatible with Make UK’s headline. It does not prove Make UK used that total. The remaining difference cannot be attributed definitively without its underlying calculation. We have not scaled our inputs to reproduce its result.

2. Establish national electricity use

We start with the Department for Energy Security and Net Zero’s Digest of UK Energy Statistics (DUKES) 2026, Table 5.2, using observed 2025 electricity supplied through the public distribution system. This is grid electricity. Electricity generated and used directly on site is excluded because it does not receive the relevant bill exemptions.

We retain the published broad industry electricity totals. ONS Annual Purchases Survey (APS) 2024 aggregate electricity expenditure helps divide each broad total between industries, and Annual Business Survey (ABS) purchases from 2017–2023 help divide them into detailed four-digit SIC classes. These detailed shares are estimates, rather than meter readings.

The earlier model treated ECUK’s detailed industry proportions as rigid controls. That produced contradictions: estimated total pharmaceutical consumption was below the government’s minimum estimate of eligible pharmaceutical consumption. We now reconcile the detailed allocations within the broad DUKES totals, ensuring they can accommodate existing support without clipping that support away.

UK consumption is adjusted to GB with the published 2025 country public-supply consumption share, applied uniformly across industries. We project to 2027/28 using DESNZ’s February 2026 Energy and Emissions Projections, Annex F: separate electricity trends for iron and steel, other industry, and commercial activity. These are forecast assumptions, not observed future consumption. The calculation and multipliers are provided in the technical note.

Water, waste and repair use an expenditure share of a broader commercial electricity control. Utilities and offshore extraction have additional coverage gaps. These limitations remain even after the arithmetic is reconciled.

Compare the latest year with a recent average

The main reference uses observed 2025 grid electricity, projected to 2027/28. We also calculate a separate comparison using the average of 2023, 2024 and 2025. All three years come from DESNZ’s DUKES 2026 Table 5.2, so they use the same revised industry definitions. We do not mix figures from older editions of DUKES.

For each broad industry, we add its grid consumption in those three years and divide by three. We then apply the same GB adjustment and the same 2025-to-2027/28 projection factor used in the main reference. This treats the recent average as an alternative starting level; it does not separately forecast each historical year before averaging. The downloadable control table shows the three annual values, their average and the projection factor.

We use the same APS 2024 and ABS industry shares to distribute those broad totals, reconciling them again to the existing BICS allocations. Repair, water and waste retain their estimated share of the broader commercial total, whose consumption is also averaged. The small metal-ore expenditure proxy has no matching annual grid series and is held fixed. Nuclear and gas retain known current support only; the existing coverage gaps remain.

The current BICS forecast stays at 15.7 TWh with the same industry split. EII support stays at 13.6 TWh. Its industry allocation is recalculated using the same rule against the alternative electricity totals, so manufacturing’s share of EII can change slightly. Both comparisons use £40/MWh, automatic full take-up and no expansion consumption threshold.

What changes for constituencies?

Each industry’s revised additional saving is distributed using exactly the same employment, regional GVA and regional expenditure weights as before. A constituency’s change therefore depends on its industrial mix; we do not increase every seat by the same national percentage. Each base has its own range across the three allocation methods. Changes are calculated by comparing the same method across bases, rather than subtracting unrelated range endpoints. Each method still adds up to its own national total.

The main headlines, map and industry breakdown retain the latest-year reference. The national and constituency comparison tables show the average separately. Averaging reduces reliance on one year, but all three years may reflect high energy prices, changing output, closures or efficiency improvements. It is not an estimate of demand returning when bills fall, a confidence interval or a resolution of the Make UK costing difference.

3. Allocate electricity to constituencies

We do not have meter readings for every factory or business. We therefore estimate where each industry's electricity is used from the location of its employees, using 2024 workplace employee data from the ONS Business Register and Employment Survey (BRES), accessed through Nomis.

Start with each constituency's share of industry employment

The calculation is made separately for every detailed industry. Under the employment method, a constituency with 2% of Great Britain's employees in an industry receives 2% of that industry's national electricity consumption. If that industry uses 100,000 MWh, the constituency is allocated 2,000 MWh.

We repeat this for every industry, then add their contributions within each constituency. This reflects the local industrial mix: an employee in an electricity-intensive industry carries a different amount of estimated electricity use from an employee in a less intensive industry. Employment is measured at workplaces, so the allocation follows where people work rather than where they live.

Adjust for regional differences in economic activity

Employment alone assumes the same electricity use per employee within an industry everywhere. We compare this with two alternatives using the 2024 ONS Annual Business Survey and matching 2024 BRES regional employment.

  • The GVA method compares regional industry output per employee with the Great Britain average. GVA, or gross value added, is a measure of economic output.
  • The expenditure method makes the same comparison using business purchases per employee.

For example, if an industry's output per employee in a region is 20% above the GB average, the GVA method multiplies local employment by 1.2 when calculating its allocation weight. A constituency with 100 employees is therefore given a weight of 120. This is a calculation weight, not an increase in actual employment.

We then divide that weight by the total of all adjusted constituency weights for the same industry. In a simplified example with two areas each employing 100 people, weights of 120 and 100 produce electricity shares of 54.5% and 45.5%, rather than 50% each. This reallocates a fixed national total; it does not create extra electricity or savings.

The regional figures cover broader, two-digit industries. Each detailed industry uses the adjustment for its broader industry and region. These are regional indicators of economic activity, not measurements of local electricity use. Where a regional adjustment is unavailable, employment allocation is retained. If an entire detailed industry has no recorded GB employment, recorded business locations or broader employment provide a documented fallback.

Check how much the regional adjustments matter

The three main views compare employment alone with the full GVA and expenditure adjustments. Supplementary tests also weaken the adjustments to see how much results depend on their strength. For example, a full factor of 1.44 is reduced to 1.20 in the intermediate test, or removed entirely in the employment-only test. Shares are recalculated to sum to 100% in every case.

These comparisons test where a fixed national benefit is allocated. They do not change national savings. Large differences indicate that a constituency estimate depends more heavily on the allocation assumption; similar results do not prove accuracy.

4. Account for current eligibility and existing support

Use the published current-support forecast

DESNZ’s September 2026 Renewables Obligation calculation forecasts 15.7 TWh of BICS-exempt electricity in 2027/28. At the exercise’s assumed £40/MWh, this is £628m across the whole scheme. It is consistent in scale with the government’s approximately £600m announcement; £40 is not an exact year-by-year levy forecast.

The government also publishes ranges of eligible electricity by detailed industry. We choose a common position within each published range so that the industries add up to the later 15.7 TWh national forecast. Manufacturing’s share is about 14.41 TWh, or £576m at £40/MWh. This industry split is our reference allocation, not a government-published central estimate for every industry.

Within each broad electricity total, we allow enough consumption to accommodate the allocated current support, then distribute the remaining electricity using the APS/ABS shares. No published support is silently clipped to fit a smaller model estimate. Where this constraint binds, a zero modelled increment does not prove that every business in the industry already receives support.

Exclude the same relief already provided through Supercharger

The same September publication forecasts 13.6 TWh of electricity already exempt under EII support in 2027/28. We allocate that amount across listed EII industries in proportion to their estimated grid electricity, limiting each allocation to electricity not already assigned to BICS. Any capped allocation is redistributed so the national total is preserved.

Manufacturing receives about 12.80 TWh of this overlap in the reference allocation. Multiplying it by £40 gives about £512m of BICS-equivalent relief that must not be counted again. That is not Supercharger’s total spending: its package also covers other charges and forms of support. We are excluding electricity already exempt from the same charges.

No minimum threshold in the expansion

The hypothetical expansion has no minimum annual electricity consumption requirement and covers all quantified grid consumption left after existing support, at automatic 100% uptake. We make no deduction for sites below 33 MWh and assume no site-consumption distribution. The baseline reflects the actual scheme’s product and site restrictions through the government’s forecast; extending eligibility can therefore benefit both newly listed industries and activities currently excluded within listed industries.

5. Calculate additional annual savings

Our financial assumptions follow the Make UK approach adopted for this exercise: £40 of relief per MWh, applied to all qualifying consumption, with automatic, 100% take-up in the expansion. Our electricity quantities remain based on the official datasets described above, rather than being adjusted to reproduce Make UK's national costing.

For each industry, we multiply grid electricity remaining after current BICS and overlapping EII support by £40 and distribute the saving between constituencies using its allocation shares. An additional 1,000 qualifying MWh therefore represents £40,000 in annual savings. We add the industry contributions to obtain each constituency's result.

The same relief rate and expansion take-up assumption apply to all constituencies, geographical methods and supplementary scenarios. The figures represent a full year's potential benefit, rather than first-year payments or likely applications.

6. Interpret the results

Without expansion and with expansion

Without expansion shows estimated annual BICS relief under current eligibility. It allocates the published 2027/28 forecast of 15.7 TWh to industries and constituencies using the same industry and geographical methods as the expansion estimates. At our assumed £40/MWh, it is worth £628m nationally. These are modelled allocations of a forecast, not observed payments or a register of participating businesses.

With expansion is current BICS relief plus the additional relief from the selected manufacturing or production expansion. It retains current support for eligible non-manufacturing activities too. Existing equivalent Supercharger relief has already been excluded when calculating the additional amount and is not subtracted a second time. The published current-scheme forecast reflects its actual eligibility restrictions, including the minimum-consumption rule; the hypothetical expansion has no minimum consumption threshold. No new site distribution or threshold adjustment is introduced.

For each constituency we calculate the combined total separately under employment, GVA and expenditure allocation, then take the lowest and highest combined estimates. We do not add the endpoints of separate ranges. The map measure changes its shading, national total, ranking and tooltips; the seat cards always show all three quantities. Industry breakdowns and the recent-average comparison continue to describe additional relief.

The results show additional savings above the published current-policy forecast. They include additional relief within listed industries, but remain incomplete where additional consumption cannot be quantified. All production includes manufacturing, so the two expansion figures should not be added together. The extra benefit of going beyond manufacturing is the production figure minus the manufacturing figure.

The industry breakdown shows which activities contribute to each constituency's additional saving. Switching between employment, GVA and expenditure shows how that estimate changes under different geographical allocations of the same national benefit.

7. Allocation sensitivity and evidence limitations

The main national reference is fixed. A separately labelled comparison uses average 2023–2025 consumption; it is not a national lower/upper range. There are no arbitrary scenarios produced by scaling consumption, Supercharger support, territorial shares or commercial consumption. The £40 rate, full take-up, no-threshold expansion policy and national industry totals are identical across the three geographical methods.

Each constituency's headline range is simply the smallest and largest of its employment, regional GVA and regional expenditure estimates. The visible table shows all three. This answers how the estimated local share changes under alternative allocation approaches. It is not a confidence interval, a complete measure of uncertainty or proof that actual savings lie within those endpoints.

For example, if the same industry contributes £100,000, £120,000 and £110,000 under the three methods, its method range is £100,000–£120,000. Constituency ranges are calculated after adding all industry contributions within each method. They are not made by selecting a different preferred method for every industry. The extra-benefit range uses the paired production-minus-manufacturing result within each method.

All methods conserve the same national industry totals. Consequently there is one national figure per expansion within each consumption base, and adding constituency range endpoints does not reproduce a national uncertainty range: different seats may reach their minimum under different methods. A national reference figure is a modelled calculation, not a claim of certainty.

A separate audit varies the allocation of existing support within published sector bounds while holding industry electricity and both national support totals fixed. This gives a conditional manufacturing range of approximately £1.316bn–£1.412bn. It is not a confidence interval and excludes other consumption, projection and coverage uncertainties. Coverage gaps: not estimated, rather than assumed to be zero

We have not established an evidence-based share of electricity-utilities operational consumption purchased from the grid, a reliable split of extraction activity between GB premises and offshore locations, or a measured split of the combined nuclear/gas/steam energy-industry budget. We therefore exclude the affected contributions from the quantified total instead of choosing unsupported percentages.

The affected detailed industries are crude petroleum and natural gas extraction (0610 and 0620), petroleum/gas extraction support (0910), nuclear-fuel processing (2446), and electricity, gas and steam utilities (3511–3530). The production reference is therefore an incomplete quantified subtotal, not the full benefit of covering every B–E activity. Known current support for nuclear fuel and gas manufacture is retained. Their additional consumption remains unestimated; this does not mean there is no further benefit. The current-support total matches the published 15.7 TWh forecast.

These activities may have genuine benefits. They are marked “not estimated”; zero values used when adding downloadable numerical tables are placeholders, not observations of zero consumption, an eligibility decision or evidence of no benefit. They must not be read as measured zero savings. Within the retained Other Fuel Industries control, the unallocated electricity could add at most about £23.3m if it all belonged to additional nuclear manufacturing consumption; this is a coverage ceiling, not a confidence interval.

Other estimates use published consumption controls, expenditure allocations and documented suppression fallbacks. Those are still proxies, not direct readings of every industry's grid purchases. The uniform UK-to-GB industry adjustment and allocation of the published Supercharger reference are also modelling assumptions. Using official inputs does not remove these limitations.

The three geographical methods share these national limitations. Their agreement cannot independently validate the national calculation or local bills. The model does not estimate jobs, investment, productivity or economic-growth effects.

8. Sources and documentation

The main reference periods are 2025 for electricity consumption; 2024 for workplace employment, regional output and purchases and Annual Purchases Survey electricity expenditure; and 2017–2023 for the Annual Business Survey data used to support detailed national industry shares.

The source register records individual datasets, their coverage and transformations. The separate evidence audit identifies what is quantified and what remains unestimated. Further assumptions, calculations and checks are available in the supporting documentation.
